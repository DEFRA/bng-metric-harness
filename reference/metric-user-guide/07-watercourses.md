## 7 Watercourse habitats only

The following sections detail specific assessment methodologies for watercourses.

### Watercourse type and distinctiveness

You should assign watercourse type using the definitions provided in table 10.

**Table 10 Watercourse distinctiveness categories**

| Watercourse type and distinctiveness | Biodiversity metric watercourse definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Priority habitat**<br>(very high distinctiveness) | Highly naturally functioning stretches which:<br>• are on the [Priority River Habitat Map](https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/20019cdb-9fef-4024-81af-daf1d1b74762/priority-river-habitat-rivers); or<br>• [meet the criteria for inclusion](https://priorityhabitats.org/contribute/contribute-naturalness-data/) |
| **Other rivers and streams**<br>(high distinctiveness) | Rivers and streams that are not classified as Priority River Habitat. You should check:<br>• [Statutory Main River Map](https://environment.maps.arcgis.com/apps/webappviewer/index.html?id=17cd53dfc524433980cc333726a56386)<br>• [Local Data on ordinary watercourses](https://www.gov.uk/find-local-council)<br>• [Internal Drainage data](https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/59af775e-efc7-458b-bdc3-593651d08aa8/association-of-drainage-authorities-administrative-boundaries-internal-drainage-districts-in-england)<br>• [MAGIC](https://magic.defra.gov.uk/MagicMap.aspx) |
| **Canals**<br>(medium distinctiveness) | An artificial body of water created for the purposes of navigation, whether it is currently navigable or not (for example, the Grand Union Canal, the Lee Navigation, River Stort Navigation)<br>Note – modified rivers (which have been straightened, over deepened or concrete lined) should not be classed as a canal and should be classed as ‘other rivers and streams’.<br>You should check:<br>• [Canal and River Network](https://canalrivertrust.org.uk/enjoy-the-waterways/canal-and-river-network)<br>• [MAGIC](https://magic.defra.gov.uk/MagicMap.aspx)<br>• Historic local data |
| **Ditch**<br>(medium distinctiveness) | Artificially created linear water-conveyancing features:<br>• Which are less than 5 metres wide; and<br>• Are likely to retain water for more than 4 months of the year<br>Do not record as a ditch if the watercourse meets the definition of:<br>• A higher distinctiveness watercourse habitat; or<br>• A canal |
| **Culvert**<br>(low distinctiveness) | A covered channel or pipe designed to prevent the obstruction of a watercourse or drainage path by an artificial construction (as defined by the [Flood and Water Management Act 2010](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/29/contents)).<br>Record culverted sections of any watercourse type as ‘culvert’.<br>A site visit may be required to identify extent of culverting. |

#### Additional information on ditches

Use Table 11 to inform classification where additional professional judgement is required (note that not each criterion has to be met to inform classification).

**Table 11 Additional information to inform habitat classification**

| Criteria | Rivers and streams (including headwaters) | Ditches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hydrology** | A feature of broader hydrological systems, including flow paths, patterns, and connections with other rivers; water levels and flows in rivers are more dynamic and less controlled, can be dry in headwaters. | A feature of localised land drainage; water levels are often artificially managed, can be dry for large parts of year.<br>Can include pumps for moving water. |
| **Landscape** | Is in hydraulic continuity with a river system (flows into / out of a river system). Origin may be traced to natural source. | Artificial feature within natural landscape; any connections with natural rivers are the result of deliberate human intervention. Cannot be traced to a natural (spring) source. |
| **Topography and gravity** | Likely to follow natural landscape contours and influenced by gravity. | Does not drain along topographic flow paths; minimal gradient. |
| **Historical** | Evidence of historical features (historic bends or loops, paleo features); course aligns with pre-existing watercourses. | Long term linear; closely aligns with field boundaries; may not be present on historic maps. |

Ordnance Survey (OS) maps, historical maps, LiDAR imagery, and topographic contours can assist in these assessments.

**Hedgerows and ditches**

Some hedgerows and lines of trees have associated ditches. If these meet the definition of a watercourse ditch, you should:

- record that ditch as a length in the watercourse module
- not use the ‘associated with ditch’ habitat types in the hedgerow module

### Watercourse sections

#### Baseline sections

Once habitat type is determined, you should walk the watercourse and use professional judgement to split a watercourse into sections.

Sections should represent different riverine and riparian characteristics, such as condition and levels of encroachment. You should:

- enter watercourse sections into separate rows in the biodiversity metric tool
- mark if a section is retained or enhanced.
- do not record partial enhancement of a section (instead split the section)

An example of baseline sections, split by condition status, is provided in figure 3.

> **[Figure:** diagram of a watercourse split into four numbered sections (350 m, 100 m, 500 m and 300 m) with a key, above an extract of the biodiversity metric showing each section as a row with its watercourse type, length, distinctiveness and condition**]**

**Figure 3** Recording sections into biodiversity metric rows

Once preliminary sections have been assigned, complete a detailed assessment of each section, collecting information on:

- length
- condition
- the riparian zone
- watercourse encroachment

You may then choose to combine adjacent sections of similar condition once you have completed a full condition assessment of your watercourse.

### Condition assessments for watercourses

Watercourse habitat types use different methodologies to assess condition, for:

- culverts – you do not need a condition assessment
- ditches – you should use biodiversity metric condition sheets
- all other watercourses (including canals) – you should conduct a river condition assessment (RCA)

You should be a qualified RCA assessor to conduct a river condition assessment.

[Find out more about RCA training.](https://modularriversurvey.org/river-condition/)

If you need to conduct an RCA, you should:

- survey at a minimum of one point for each section
- survey a length at least 20% of the total length of the watercourse assessed
- survey at points of known intervention (where possible)

### The riparian zone

#### Defining the riparian zone

The riparian zone is a set area from the bank top of the watercourse. The bank top is where there is a break in slope between the river channel and the surrounding land. Figure 4 shows an illustration of a 10 metre riparian zone (highlighted in blue).

> **[Figure:** photo of a ditch with a 10 m riparian zone highlighted in blue on each side, measured from the bank top**]**

**Figure 4** A photo showing a 10 m riparian zone starting from edge of the bank top.

The riparian zone supports features which influence the hydrological, geomorphological and biological functions or processes within the channel. It also provides ecological function for riparian or aquatic species.

Different watercourse habitat types have different riparian zone widths. Table 12 sets out riparian zone widths for different watercourse habitat types.

**Table 12: Riparian zone widths for watercourse habitat types**

| Habitat Type | Riparian Zone Width |
| --- | --- |
| **Priority rivers** | 10 metres from the top of each bank |
| **Other rivers and streams** | 10 metres from the top of each bank |
| **Canals** | 10 metres from the top of each bank |
| **Ditches** | 5 metres from the top of each bank |
| **Culverts** | Not applicable |

#### Works within a riparian zone

Works to the riparian zone may result in changes to the:

- quality of terrestrial habitats found within the site boundary
- condition of the watercourse through the RCA
- severity of riparian zone encroachment

Any works within the riparian zone should be assessed in the area and hedgerow modules (e.g. changes from excavated or removed watercourse channels).

Works within the riparian zone may also result in loss or gain of watercourse units through changes to condition and riparian zone encroachment.

If the site boundary crosses into the riparian zone, you should:

- include adjacent lengths of watercourse in the watercourse module
- record and assess terrestrial habitats within the area and hedgerow modules if they are within the site boundary

You will need to review riparian zone features across both banks. This includes areas of riparian zone outside of the site boundary. This includes an:

- assessment of the riparian zone vegetation as part of the RCA
- assessment of riparian zone encroachment

#### Riparian zone encroachment

Riparian zone encroachment is any feature or intervention (within the riparian zone) that reduces the quantity, quality or ecological function of the riparian habitat. This includes:

- buildings or hardstanding
- management practice (including agriculture)
- structures that prevent wildlife from accessing the riverbank

The following features are exceptions and should not be recorded in the baseline:

- established canal or river navigation towpaths
- established footpaths and existing river crossings
- existing small amenity features and utility units where the total footprint is less than 5% of the riparian zone area

The footprint of riparian zone encroachment is the cumulative area of these features or interventions.

Use definitions provided in table 13 to assess encroachment. The assessment can be conducted from either bank. You should:

- conduct an assessment for each watercourse section
- assess the left-hand bank
- assess the right-hand bank

Enter the combination of results within the biodiversity metric tool for each watercourse section. For example, if encroachment was major on the left bank, and major on the right bank, you should select the ‘Major / Major’ option.

**Table 13 Watercourse riparian zone encroachment bands**

| Riparian zone encroachment band for a bank | Definition for rivers and canals | Definition for Ditches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **No encroachment** | No encroachment within 10 metres of bank top | No encroachment within 5 metres of bank top |
| **Minor** | Any encroachment 8 to 10 metres from the bank top (covering up to 100% of area); or<br>where the footprint of encroachment occupies 0-10% of the riparian zone area 4 to 10 metres from bank top. | Any encroachment 4 to 5 metres from the bank top (covering up to 100% of area); or<br>where the footprint of encroachment occupies 0-10% of the riparian zone area 2 to 5 metres from bank top. |
| **Moderate** | Where the footprint of encroachment occupies between 10% to 25% of the riparian zone area 4 to 10 metres from the bank top. | Where the footprint of encroachment occupies between 10% to 25% of the riparian zone area 2 to 5 metres from the bank top. |
| **Major** | Any encroachment 0 to 4 metres from the bank top; or<br>Where encroachment occupies greater than 25% of the total riparian zone area 4 to 10 metres from the bank top. | Any encroachment 0 to 2 metres from the bank top; or<br>Where encroachment occupies greater than 25% of the total riparian zone area 2 to 5 metres from the bank top. |

### Watercourse encroachment

Watercourse encroachment can be any feature or action that adversely affects the natural function of the watercourse, or results in localised changes in habitat, species and migratory pathways.

The watercourse encroachment multiplier accounts for development within a riverbank or channel that impacts the function of the river corridor.

Use the descriptions in table 14 to assign a watercourse encroachment band for each watercourse section entered within the biodiversity metric tool.  You should assess encroachment at baseline and at post-intervention for each section.

**Table 14 Watercourse encroachment bands**

| Watercourse encroachment band | Multiplier | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **No encroachment** | 1.0 | less than 5% of the bank length is an engineered bank revetment and there is no encroachment into the channel | N/A |
| **Minor** | 0.8 | 5% to 20% of the bank length is an engineered bank revetment; or<br>there is encroachment across up to 10% of the channel width at any one point | Small headwalls, jetties, pontoons |
| **Major** | 0.5 | greater than 20% of the bank length is an engineered bank revetment; or<br>there is encroachment across greater than 10% of the channel width at any one point | Weirs, large headwalls, bank revetment |
| **N/A - Culvert** | 0.68 | to be used for culverts only | Culverts |

You should not record features that are, or have been, introduced to restore the ‘condition’ of the river, reinstate natural riverine processes, or improve migratory pathways as encroachment. Examples include woody beaver dams and soft bank revetment such as coir rolls, willow spiling or floating islands and within river fish passes (such as rock ramps).

#### Partial removal of watercourse encroachment

In scenarios where either 20% engineered bank revetment, or in-channel structures, cannot be taken out (due to structural reasons or designated use) it may only be possible to action partial restoration of the watercourse.

If these restrictions apply, but action is taken to reduce either the extent of bank revetment or remove an in-channel structure, you may reduce the encroachment multiplier from major to minor within the enhancement tab of the biodiversity metric tool (for example, a weir is removed but not bank revetment for structural reasons).

#### Technical fish passes

In scenarios where a technical fish pass (a fish pass that is attached to an existing weir/structure) is the only feasible enhancement option you may reduce the watercourse encroachment multiplier from major to minor within the enhancement tab.

### Defining watercourse interventions

Use the definitions and descriptions in the sections below to determine if retention, creation or enhancement is applicable to the scheme, and whether to record loss at baseline.

You should consider how the intervention impacts the natural function of the watercourse when deciding which intervention to choose.

#### Watercourse retention

Watercourse retention is where there are no interventions on the watercourse or riparian zone.

#### Watercourse enhancement

A watercourse is enhanced where the project interventions promote natural function, processes, and the development of natural habitats. Examples include:

- removing culverts
- restoring natural alignment
- enhancing the riparian zone
- removal of encroachment features
- removal of tidal structures and restoration of tidal processes

You should record increases in the baseline condition, distinctiveness, length or reduction in encroachment, as an enhancement of the baseline watercourse section. You may also:

- split enhanced sections into multiple rows in the metric
- retain RCA information for each split section
- consider if the intervention will also result in impacts to the condition of upstream and downstream sections

Enhanced sections may be longer than baseline sections. For example, where meanders, channels or braiding are included. If the watercourse is re-meandered you should:

- not record any loss of watercourse length
- split the baseline section into a retained and enhanced sections (if applicable)
- record the total enhanced length within the enhanced section

Re-meandering may also result in redundant former watercourse channels.  For example, if you create wet woodland in a redundant channel, record the creation of wet woodland within the area module. Record these changes in area habitats within the area module.

#### Watercourse creation

If your watercourse interventions do not promote natural functions and processes, or the development of natural watercourse habitats, then this is creation. Examples include:

- installing culverts
- trapezoidal channels
- channel straightening
- physical modification
- altering natural alignment
- increases in encroachment

Any interventions that do not promote natural functions and processes should be represented through a recorded decrease in baseline condition, length or distinctiveness of natural river, or a recorded increase in encroachment. You should:

- split any impacted length of a section into a separate row in the metric
- retain RCA information for any split sections
- record a loss of the impacted watercourse section
- record post-intervention lengths within the creation tab reflecting any decreases in condition, length, distinctiveness, or increase in encroachment

You should also consider if the intervention will result in impacts to the condition of upstream and downstream sections.

Creation can also be the installation of a new length of canal or ditch (with no associated loss).

You should record post-intervention lengths within the creation tab. Creation in advance may be set to 10+ years if there are increases in encroachment with no other impacts.

#### Permanent loss of watercourses

Permanent loss of a watercourse length only applies to canals or ditches (natural rivers cannot be permanently lost).

### Other notes on watercourses

#### Estuaries

Sub-tidal reaches are not included within the watercourse module of the biodiversity metric tool. You can apply the watercourse biodiversity metric if the sub-tidal estuary reaches show riverine features (predominantly in the upper sections of transitional areas between rivers and estuaries).

Water Framework Directive (WFD) data sets show the boundary between riverine and subtidal estuarine reaches. You can use this to inform discussion with the relevant decision maker.

[Find out more about the WFD Transitional and Coastal Waterbodies Cycle 2](https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/3a75ec5f-a361-475c-80e3-52d93bbc5dbe/wfd-transitional-and-coastal-waterbodies-cycle-2)

#### Weir removal

Where appropriately evidenced, you may assess major engineering works that will significantly influence the watercourse as ‘off-site’ if beyond the project boundary. An example is the removal of a weir.
